In a small workshop in Asheville, Henry Bennett is finishing what he says will be the last birdhouses he ever makes.

At 78, his hands are not what they used to be. The grip that once held a chisel steady for hours now trembles after twenty minutes. But the birds don’t know that. They arrive every spring, looking for a safe place to raise their young.

“It’s the silence,” he says. “That’s what bothers me.”

A Gradual Change Few People Notice

Across the country, hummingbird populations have been quietly declining. It’s not a dramatic collapse — nothing that makes the evening news. It’s a gradual fading. Fewer flashes of iridescent green at the feeder. Fewer tiny nests tucked under porch eaves.

Researchers point to habitat loss as the primary driver. As neighborhoods expand and old trees are cleared for new construction, the natural cavities that hummingbirds rely on for nesting disappear.

“Twenty years ago, when I opened the workshop in the morning, they were always there. Now, some mornings… there’s nothing.”

What’s changed:

  • ✘ Old trees with natural cavities are removed.
  • ✘ Gardens are simplified.
  • ✘ New buildings leave fewer gaps or sheltered areas.

Most of Them Are Not Built for Birds

Henry shakes his head when he walks past the garden section of a hardware store. Most of what he sees there, he says, was designed to catch a human’s eye — not house a bird.

The common problems he sees:

  • ❌ Entrance holes too large.
  • ❌ Treated wood.
  • ❌ Walls too thin.
  • ❌ No ventilation, no drainage.

How He Learned What Works

Henry didn’t start out as a birdhouse maker. He was a carpenter for forty years — cabinets, furniture, the occasional deck. But when he noticed the hummingbirds disappearing from his own yard, he decided to do something about it.

That was over two decades ago. Since then, he’s built and tested hundreds of prototypes. Different sizes. Different wood types. Different entrance diameters. Different roof angles. He kept notes on everything — which designs the birds actually used, and which ones they ignored.

The result is a birdhouse that took twenty years to perfect. Not because it’s complicated — but because getting the details right for a creature that weighs less than a nickel requires patience most people don’t have.
